01

Business Analysis — What This Company Actually Is

The Business Intelligence section frames this as three divisions. That is accurate as an org chart and misleading as a strategy. Read the raw interview and a different, sharper business appears.

This is not a lighting company that also does electrical and construction. It is an access-and-accountability business that happens to express itself through lighting, electrical, and construction.

Three forms of access make the money:

1 · Access to product at distributor pricing

"Buying a light fixture package from us is beautiful business. I don't even have to mess with it… I buy the package at $20,000 and I sell it for $32,000. Happy. I flipped it. Never even touch it."

Owner interview

This is the highest-margin activity described anywhere in the interview — roughly 60% markup with no labour, no crew, no callback risk. It is also completely invisible on both current websites and has no brand name. A buyer today cannot tell that they can purchase a lighting package from this company without hiring them to install it. That is a revenue line hiding in plain sight, and fixing it is close to free.

2 · Access to gatekept commercial properties

"When you walk into a building, you need to be vetted through one of the compliance people. They're either Compliance Depot, [NetVendor], … First Service Residential… That's what I deal with a lot."

Owner interview

Being an approved vendor inside property-management compliance networks is slow, expensive, and paperwork-heavy to obtain — which is exactly what makes it a moat. Most competitors in this space cannot get in the door at all. Almost none of them advertise the fact that they can. A dedicated property-manager pathway on the website, with the compliance paperwork ready to download, would be genuinely differentiated in this category.

3 · Access to vetted trades — sold as accountability

"You got to pick and choose the right guy. I got to vet him. I don't have to deal with a headache and my customer doesn't have to deal with it. I deal with it."

Owner interview

This is the single most useful sentence in the transcript. The product being sold is not lighting, electrical, or construction. It is the removal of coordination burden. A commercial property owner running a buildout is managing six vendors, six schedules, six invoices, and six opportunities for someone to blame someone else. The offer here is: one number, one accountable party, one company that absorbs the problem.

That reframing matters because it solves the central risk of the whole project. "We do lighting and electrical and construction and sourcing and maintenance" normally reads as handyman — an unfocused generalist. "One accountable partner, and here is the depth behind each capability" reads as a firm. Same facts, opposite impression.

The fourth line: recurring maintenance — not yet real

The quarterly landscape-lighting service program is described in the interview as an intention, not an operating product: "that's one of the things that we've been trying to work on for a really long time, but we're like so busy that we can't even wrap our…". It is strategically the best idea in the business — recurring revenue against an installed base they already own — but the website must present it as a program you can register interest in, not a service with published terms, until it actually exists and is staffed.

Strategic read

The one-stop-shop story in the brief is right, but it is being justified with the wrong argument. "We can do all of it" is a capability claim, and capability claims invite doubt. "We are the one party accountable for all of it" is a risk claim, and risk is what commercial buyers actually shop on. Same architecture, much stronger frame.

Brand Architecture

Research turned up a structural problem the brief does not mention: there are at least two live websites, and the parent company's domain is behaving like a division page.

PropertyWhat the index showsProblem
worldwidedistributorsinc.com Page title reads "A Division of Worldwide Distributors Inc." Thin content: 30-years claim, LED-transition copy, a generic "drop us a line" contact form. The parent domain presents itself as its own subsidiary. Architecturally inverted, and the thinnest of the two properties.
elighting.org Substantially deeper: /about-us, /products, /landscape, /landscape-lighting, /studio-and-stage-lighting, /matrix-page, /contact. Showroom address, hours, warranty and certification claims. The division carries the brand equity, the content, and probably the search authority. The umbrella does not.
@elightingdesigns 802 posts. Bio: residential & commercial lighting, free energy survey, a phone number. Third identity, third phone number, no connection back to the umbrella.

Brand equity, search authority, and customer memory are split three ways, and the strongest asset sits on the division rather than the parent. Any rebuild has to resolve this before it does anything else.

Three options

A · Branded house

Rejected

Rename everything under Worldwide (Worldwide Lighting / Electric / Build). Clean, but it destroys E-Lighting's name recognition, its domain, its Instagram, and whatever search history it holds. Too expensive for the tidiness gained.

B · House of brands

Rejected

Three separate sites, three identities. This is effectively the current state, and it is the thing that is not working — it fragments SEO and makes the one-stop-shop story impossible to tell.

C · Endorsed house

Recommended

One domain, one site. E-Lighting and E-Built live as first-class sections inside it, each with its own landing page, accent colour, and conversion path, each signed "A division of Worldwide Distributors."

Option C keeps the divisional names as proof of depth while consolidating every visitor, every backlink and every conversion into one property. The divisions stop being separate companies and start being evidence that the umbrella is real.

Name the third division

The distribution arm — the "$20,000 in, $32,000 out, never touched it" business — has no name. That asymmetry is why it keeps disappearing from the story. Give it one and the architecture becomes legible in a single glance:

Worldwide SupplySource & supply — lighting packages, fixtures, doors, windows, materials. Sold with or without installation.
E-LightingDesign, electrical contracting, installation, service, maintenance.
E-BuiltCommercial buildouts, tenant improvements, remodelling, construction.

Worldwide Supply is a placeholder for approval, not a final name. The structural point stands regardless of what it gets called: three symmetrical divisions covering supply, install, and build, under one parent.

Verify · Domain consolidation

Which domain becomes the primary is a data question I cannot answer from here. If elighting.org holds materially more search authority than worldwidedistributorsinc.com, the right move may be to build on the E-Lighting domain and position Worldwide Distributors as the parent brand within it. I need Search Console data for both, plus confirmation that you control both registrations, before recommending direction. Whichever way it goes, the other domain gets 301-redirected page-by-page rather than switched off.

Verify · Name collision risk

"Worldwide Distributors" is a crowded name. Search surfaces a Worldwide Distribution LLLP in Jacksonville, a separate Worldwide Distributors Inc. listed in Dunnellon FL, and a Worldwide Lighting Corp — none of which appear to be you. This makes the parent name hard to own in search and easy to confuse. I am not recommending a rename; that is your call and outside this scope. I am recommending we design around it: lead with capability and location, never with the bare name, and never publish a claim sourced from one of those other entities.

Verify · E-Built has no public existence

I found no website, no licence record, no social presence, and no directory listing for E-Built. Treat this as a brand launch, not a refresh — which changes the content plan significantly, because there is no existing E-Built photography, copy, or proof to inherit. Confirm whether E-Built is a registered entity, what it operates under, and who the qualifying licence holder is.

The competitive field around this business splits into four groups that do not overlap:

General contractorsFragmented local marketSubcontract lighting and electrical out; no sourcing advantage

No competitor found spans all four. That gap is real and defensible — and it is the entire strategic argument for this website.

The danger is equally real. Breadth claims read as weakness unless they are backed by proof density. A visitor who reads "lighting, electrical, and construction" and then finds three shallow paragraphs concludes you dabble. A visitor who reads the same headline and then finds three specialist-grade sections — real projects, real process, real depth — concludes you are a firm with divisions. The headline is identical. The proof is what decides which conclusion they reach.

So the positioning statement I would build toward:

Recommended positioning

For commercial property owners, managers, and tenants in Florida, Worldwide Distributors is the single accountable partner for everything between an empty space and a finished, lit, working building — because it sources the product, designs the lighting, performs the electrical, builds the space, and maintains it afterward, rather than coordinating four companies who each blame the other three.

Visual Direction

The brief asks for cinematic, premium, architectural. The trap in that request is the default contractor-premium look — black background, neon accent, chrome gradients — which reads as cheap precisely because everyone reaches for it.

The direction I would take instead: light is the medium, so the palette should be built from real colour temperature. Landscape and architectural lighting lives between 2700K and 3000K. That warm tungsten is the company's actual product, on every job, in every photograph. Making it the brand accent means the site and the work are the same colour.

Palette

Primary ground

Division accents keep the three sections distinguishable without fracturing the identity: E-Lighting takes warm tungsten; E-Built takes concrete grey with a narrow safety-orange marker; Supply takes the cool signal blue. Same system, three temperatures.

Typography

An expanded grotesk for display — signage-derived, architectural, set tight and heavy — paired with a technical sans for body and a monospace for specifications, project codes, and data. Deliberately not the condensed athletic face that every contractor site uses, and not an editorial serif, which would read residential-boutique and undercut the commercial positioning.

Photography

Night-heavy, wide, architectural framing. Contrast is the brand — a lit building against a dark sky is the product. Two hard rules: no stock imagery anywhere, and every image labelled by type. The brief is right to insist on this, and I would make it a build-level constraint rather than an editorial guideline:

Build rule · AI labelling

Every image on the site carries a required mediaType field — Project Photography, Conceptual Rendering, AI Visualization, or Proposed Design — enforced in the content schema so an unlabelled image cannot be published. Renderings and AI visualisations render with a persistent visible caption, not a hover state. This is a legal protection as much as an ethical one, and it should be impossible to bypass by accident.

Motion

One governing metaphor: things do not slide or fade in — they illuminate. Content arrives as though a fixture just switched on: a fast warm-up curve, a slight bloom, then settle. Used consistently, that single idea does more brand work than a dozen unrelated effects, and it costs almost nothing in performance. Everything respects prefers-reduced-motion.

Content Strategy

Priority 1 — mine what exists

Triage the 802 Instagram posts before commissioning anything. Target: 12–15 projects with enough material to become real case studies, and 30–40 supporting images for service pages.

Priority 2 — the case study template

Every project follows one structure so they accumulate into a system rather than a scrapbook: the space as found → the problem → what we sourced → what we built → the electrical and lighting → the finished result → the result at night → what the client said. That structure also happens to be the buildout narrative the brief describes, which means every case study restates the one-stop-shop argument without ever asserting it.

Priority 3 — what must be filmed new

  • Hero footage, if we commit to a motion hero (section 13)
  • 2–3 video testimonials — the owner named specific willing customers in the interview
  • One buildout followed end to end, which becomes the flagship case study and doubles as educational content
  • Owner-to-camera pieces for /learn — these can be shot on a phone in the field and should be, because polish is not what makes them credible

Priority 4 — the educational hub

The interview says the owners want to make educational video. That intention aligns exactly with the SEO strategy, which is unusually convenient. Strongest starting topics, chosen for search demand and sales utility: what a landscape lighting maintenance plan actually includes · when a panel needs upgrading · what a commercial buildout really costs in time · LED retrofit payback maths · why landscape lighting fails after two years · what property managers should require from a lighting vendor.

Content system requirement

These are working field contractors. If publishing a project requires a laptop and fifteen minutes, it will not happen, and the site will be frozen in six months. The admin must accept a phone photo dump plus four or five fields, and do the rest automatically. This is a genuine build requirement, not a nice-to-have — it determines whether the whole content strategy survives launch.

SEO Strategy

Verify · Service area conflict

This is the most consequential conflict I found between the brief and the evidence. The brief instructs that statewide Florida service be communicated prominently. Every verifiable trace points to Miami-Dade: a Kendall showroom, projects in Doral and Ocean Reef, a 305 number. The interview says only "I'm from here, all the way north", which is ambiguous.

Claiming "All of Florida" from a single South Florida location does two kinds of damage: a Tampa or Jacksonville commercial buyer will not believe it, and it dilutes the local search signal that generates the highest-intent traffic you have. My recommendation is "Headquartered in Miami. Serving Florida." with an honest tiered service area. If there are crews, offices, or licences outside South Florida, tell me and I will build the statewide architecture properly.

Sequence

  1. Fix the foundation first. One name, one address, one phone, everywhere. Consolidate the domains with page-level 301s. Claim and complete the Google Business Profile.
  2. Geographic tiers. Tier 1 — Miami-Dade (Miami, Kendall, Coral Gables, Doral, Pinecrest, Coconut Grove, Key Biscayne, Aventura). Tier 2 — Broward and Palm Beach. Tier 3 — statewide and the Keys, for commercial and supply only, where the model genuinely travels.
  3. Service × audience × geography pages, built only where real demand supports them.
  4. Topical authority through /learn.

Conversion Strategy

Today there is one generic form serving buyers whose deal sizes differ by two orders of magnitude. Replace it with five routed intents, each with its own qualifying questions, its own destination, and its own response commitment.

PathAudienceQualifiers captured
Commercial buildout consultationTenants, developers, GCsSpace type, square footage, lease/permit status, target opening date
Property manager servicesPMs, associations, multifamilyPortfolio size, property type, compliance network, recurring vs. one-off

The third path is the important one. It makes the highest-margin activity in the business — the untouched package flip — orderable for the first time. Right now a general contractor who would happily buy $32,000 of fixtures has no way to discover that they can.

Supporting mechanics: sticky call button on mobile throughout; the vendor-info page as a frictionless download for PMs; a maintenance-program waitlist rather than a purchase; and a published response-time commitment — but only one the team will actually hit, since "underpromise, overdeliver" stops being a philosophy the moment the website overpromises on their behalf.

13

Cinematic Concepts

Concept 1 · First Light

The brief's own concept, tightened. A single property at dusk, scroll-scrubbed into full night. Fixtures ignite in sequence as the visitor scrolls — path lights, then uplighting on the facade, then interior, then the sign. Copy layers reveal in step with each zone.

Strength: emotionally the most beautiful, and it shows the product doing its job. Weakness: it only tells the lighting story. A visitor could still leave thinking "these guys sell lights" — the exact reaction the brief says to avoid. Best used: as the E-Lighting division hero rather than the homepage.

Concept 2 · The Spine Recommended

One continuous camera move, scroll-scrubbed, through six states without a cut: a pallet breaking open in the warehouse → a design table with a lighting plan → the truck loading → an install in progress → a framed buildout with conduit exposed → the same space finished and lit at night.

Why this one: it is Source → Design → Supply → Install → Build → Maintain, delivered as an experience rather than a claim. The strongest strategic idea in the business becomes the literal mechanic of the page. A visitor who scrolls ten seconds cannot come away thinking this is a lighting store. Cost: requires a planned shoot across multiple locations, matched for continuity.

Concept 3 · Split State Recommended as section mechanic

A vertical seam down the viewport. Left: dark, empty, unbuilt. Right: lit, finished, working. The seam tracks scroll position or drag. Applied across the site — day/night on landscape projects, empty/finished on buildouts, before/after on electrical.

Why it matters: it needs only still photographs, which means it works with the library that already exists. Extremely fast, excellent on mobile, and it demonstrates transformation more directly than video because the visitor controls the reveal.

Concept 4 · The Facade

A dark building elevation of window tiles. Each window illuminates on hover or in sequence, revealing a different project behind it. Clicking enters that case study.

Why it is here: a portfolio-first interaction that is also a lighting metaphor, built entirely from stills. Strong candidate for the /projects index page.

Production reality — please read

Concepts 1 and 2 require footage that does not exist yet. The failure mode of a cinematic brief is a beautiful shell full of placeholders that never gets filled, and then a site that cannot launch.

My recommendation: build the hero as a swappable slot. Launch on Concepts 3 and 4, which look genuinely excellent using material you already have. Shoot Concept 2 properly, without schedule pressure, and drop it into the same slot when it is ready — no rebuild, no redesign, no second invoice for structure. You get a site that ships and a site that ends up cinematic, rather than choosing between them.

Technical Approach

The working repository is already scaffolded on a stack that suits this project well: React 19, Vite 7, Hono on Cloudflare Workers, D1 with Drizzle, Tailwind v4. Cloudflare's edge network is a genuine advantage for a media-heavy site that must feel instant, and Workers plus D1 handles lead capture without a separate backend. I would keep the stack and strip the unrelated application code currently in it.

The one real technical risk

The scaffold uses client-side routing. For a site whose entire commercial case rests on Florida search visibility, shipping an empty HTML shell that requires JavaScript to render is the single most damaging decision available. Recommendation: add build-time prerendering so every route ships as real crawlable HTML while keeping the client-side interactivity the cinematic direction needs. It is the lowest-risk path to both. If the content volume later justifies it, moving to full server rendering on Workers is a contained change, not a rebuild.
